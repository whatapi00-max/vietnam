Vietnam Compliance Checklist for Chinese Investors: Legal Representative Status, District Transfer Tax Clearance, and E-Identity Before Restructuring

Why Chinese-Background FDI Restructurings in Vietnam Run Into Specific Compliance Challenges

China remains one of the largest sources of foreign direct investment into Vietnam, spanning manufacturing, electronics, retail, and services. As Chinese investors’ Vietnam operations mature — moving beyond the initial establishment phase into operational expansion, restructuring, or capital structure optimisation — they frequently encounter a cluster of compliance issues that are particularly associated with how Chinese-background FDI operations are typically structured and managed.

The three most recurring issues that Vietoria identifies when advising on Chinese-background restructuring mandates are: the legal representative’s administrative documentation status (work permit or exemption, TRC, e-identity); the tax clearance requirement when changing the company’s registered address across district lines; and the accounting record accuracy that determines whether tax clearance can actually be obtained within the restructuring timeline. Each issue can halt a restructuring that should take weeks into one that takes months.

Issue 1: The Legal Representative Without Valid Administrative Documentation

The legal representative (người đại diện theo pháp luật) of a Vietnam company has broad authority to sign contracts, applications, regulatory filings, and corporate resolutions on behalf of the company. When the legal representative is a foreign national — including a Chinese citizen appointed by the Chinese parent company — that individual is required to hold either a valid work permit (giấy phép lao động) or a formal work permit exemption confirmation, a valid Temporary Residence Card (Thẻ tạm trú — TRC) or appropriate visa, and — increasingly in Vietnam’s e-government framework — a Chip-based Electronic Identity Card (thẻ căn cước / e-identity card) or its foreign national equivalent, which is used to authenticate digital signatures and portal access.

In practice, Vietoria frequently encounters Chinese legal representatives who were appointed on the initial ERC but have allowed their work permit and TRC to lapse — or who never formally applied for a work permit in the first instance, relying on a short-term business visa. These individuals may have been signing corporate documents and submitting regulatory applications for months or years without the required administrative documentation. All documents signed by a foreign legal representative without a valid work permit or TRC are potentially challengeable as invalid — which means IRC amendment applications, tax declarations, customs import applications, and commercial contracts signed in that period are all affected.

Before initiating any restructuring that requires the legal representative to sign new applications or resolutions, the first step is to verify the legal representative’s current status: is the work permit current, is the TRC current, and has an e-identity card or equivalent been obtained where digital authentication is required? If the work permit has lapsed, a new application or renewal must be completed before the restructuring applications are submitted. If there is a prior period during which the legal representative operated without valid documentation, a legal review of the affected documents and the approach to regularisation should precede any new applications to the licensing or tax authorities.

Issue 2: The District Transfer Requires Tax Clearance at the Old District First

When a Vietnam company changes its registered business address from one administrative district to another — for example, moving from District 1 in HCMC to Thu Duc City (the merged administrative unit that encompasses the former Districts 2, 9, and Thu Duc) — the company must complete a formal address change process with both the enterprise registration authority and the tax authority. For a foreign-invested enterprise, this also involves IRC amendment at the investment registration authority.

The element of this process that most Chinese-background restructurings do not anticipate is the tax clearance requirement. Before the old district’s tax authority will issue the transfer confirmation that allows the new district to accept the company’s registration, the old district’s tax authority must confirm that the company has no outstanding tax obligations: all tax declarations up to the transfer date must be filed, any outstanding tax payable must be settled, and any pending tax audit or inspection at the old district must be resolved or formally transferred to the new district’s tax authority.

The practical consequence is that a company with unresolved tax filing gaps — missing VAT declarations for recent months, outstanding PIT finalisation, or a pending CIT inspection from a prior year — cannot complete the district transfer until those issues are resolved. The old district’s tax authority will not issue the transfer confirmation, and the new district will not register the company without it. The restructuring that was planned to take six to eight weeks extends indefinitely while the tax gap is resolved.

Issue 3: Accumulated Accounting Discrepancies That Freeze the Tax Clearance

The most time-consuming element of the district transfer tax clearance process is typically not the filing of current-period declarations — it is the discovery and remediation of prior-period accounting errors that were never individually significant enough to trigger a formal inspection, but that have accumulated into a material reconciliation gap between the books and the tax filings.

Common accounting discrepancies in Chinese-background Vietnam operations include: inter-company transactions (including payments to the Chinese parent for management fees, royalties, or shared services) that are not properly documented for FCT and transfer pricing purposes; VAT input credits claimed on invoices that do not meet the Vietnamese VAT invoice validity requirements; fixed asset registers that do not reconcile to the customs import declarations for production machinery (because some machinery was brought in without proper customs registration); and payroll records where cash payments to Chinese employees working in Vietnam have not been properly reflected in the PIT declarations.

When the tax clearance process begins, the old district’s tax authority typically conducts a desk review of the company’s recent tax filings as part of issuing the transfer confirmation. Discrepancies identified in this review must be resolved — either through explanation and supplementary documentation, or through amended declarations and payment of outstanding amounts — before clearance can be issued. The timeline for this resolution depends on the complexity and extent of the discrepancies. Companies with clean books complete district transfers in six to ten weeks. Companies with accumulated discrepancies commonly take four to six months or longer.

The Recommended Pre-Restructuring Compliance Sequence

For Chinese-background Vietnam operations planning a restructuring that involves any of the following — change of legal representative, change of registered address across district lines, capital structure change, or significant IRC amendment — Vietoria recommends completing a compliance verification exercise before the restructuring applications are filed. The verification exercise covers: legal representative documentation status (work permit, TRC, e-identity current and valid); accounting reconciliation (books vs. tax filings for the most recent three years, with specific focus on inter-company transactions, FCT, and payroll PIT); customs records for production machinery; and outstanding regulatory filings or notices from any government authority.

The verification exercise is designed to identify issues that would block or delay the restructuring, and to resolve them before the restructuring timeline begins — rather than discovering them mid-process when the clock is already running.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the district transfer requirement apply to a change of address within the same district?

No. An address change within the same district — for example, moving from one office building to another in the same district — does not trigger the inter-district tax clearance requirement. The company must still notify the enterprise registration authority and update the ERC and IRC, but the tax authority transfer process described in this article applies only to cross-district address changes where the administering tax sub-department (Chi cục Thuế) changes.

Is there a way to accelerate the district transfer tax clearance process?

The most reliable way to accelerate the process is to begin it with clean books — complete filings for all recent periods, no outstanding declarations, and accurate accounting records. Where discrepancies exist, the second-best approach is to identify and resolve them proactively before the clearance application is filed, rather than responding to the tax authority’s review findings after the process has started. A legal or tax representative who has an existing working relationship with the relevant tax sub-department can also help manage the administrative timeline and ensure that the company’s responses to any review questions are submitted promptly and completely.

What is the e-identity card requirement for foreign legal representatives in Vietnam?

Vietnam’s e-government framework increasingly requires digital authentication for portal submissions, including investment monitoring report filings, tax declaration systems, and customs clearance portals. Foreign nationals can obtain a Chip-based Temporary Residence Card that functions as their e-identity credential for these systems. The process for obtaining this credential is managed by the immigration authority. Foreign legal representatives who do not yet have e-identity credentials cannot independently complete portal-based filings and must use a tax agent or legal representative for system submissions — which adds a layer of cost and coordination to ongoing compliance management.

Can the Chinese parent company appoint a Vietnamese citizen as legal representative to avoid the work permit and TRC requirement?

Yes — appointing a Vietnamese citizen as the legal representative eliminates the work permit, TRC, and e-identity complexity for the legal representative role. Many Chinese-background FIEs use this structure, with the Vietnamese legal representative acting on operational instructions from the Chinese parent under a management services arrangement. This structure works well for routine operations and compliance filings. However, when decisions require the principal business judgment of the Chinese investor — capital changes, major contracts, IRC amendments — the legal documentation must still reflect who has actual authority, and the Vietnamese legal representative’s authority must be clearly defined and bounded to avoid governance conflicts.
