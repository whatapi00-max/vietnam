Company Incorporation in Vietnam

By Vu Manh Quynh, Managing Partner, Attorney-at-Law · Published 26 June 2026

Incorporation is not a filing exercise — it is the first strategic decision a foreign investor makes in Vietnam, and it is far cheaper to get right on day one than to unwind later. Vietoria structures each new entity around the client’s actual commercial plan, not a template, because the choices made at this stage lock in tax treatment, control rights, and exit flexibility for years to come.

Choosing your entity type before you choose your address

Many investors approach incorporation in the wrong order: they secure a site or a landlord letter of intent, then try to fit a legal structure around it. This is backwards. The entity type and ownership structure should be settled first, because they determine what sector conditions apply, what capital contribution schedule is realistic, and what governance rights the investor actually needs. A limited liability company suits investors who want a closed ownership circle and simpler internal governance, while a joint-stock company becomes necessary once share transferability, multiple future investors, or a public listing pathway are on the horizon. Within an LLC, the single-member versus multi-member choice affects everything from decision-making quorum to how easily new capital can be brought in later — a detail that is often only discovered when a second investor wants in and the charter has to be reworked from scratch.

Wholly foreign-owned vs. joint-venture structures

Vietnam’s default position for most business lines allows full foreign ownership, but a meaningful subset of sectors — logistics, distribution and retail, education, certain media and advertising activities, and others — carry conditions that cap foreign equity, require a local partner, or impose additional approval steps. The practical difficulty is that these conditions are not always obvious from the business line description alone; the same investor activity can fall inside or outside a restricted category depending on how it is scoped in the application. Getting the sector classification wrong at the outset creates two bad outcomes: either a rejected application that costs months, or an approved license that is narrower than the business actually needs, forcing a costly amendment down the line. Where a joint-venture structure is unavoidable, the shareholder or member agreement matters as much as the license itself — it should anticipate deadlock scenarios, exit mechanics, and how future foreign capital increases will be handled, rather than treating the local partner relationship as a formality to satisfy on day one.

The IRC/ERC registration sequence in practice

The Investment Registration Certificate and Enterprise Registration Certificate are sequential, not parallel, and each has its own document set, review body, and points of friction. The IRC stage is where the investment project itself is assessed — proposed capital, business lines, and location — and it is where sector-specific conditions and sub-licensing requirements surface first. Only once the IRC is granted does the ERC stage formally establish the company as a legal entity with a tax code and seal. Investors who treat these as a single administrative step often underestimate how much the IRC application’s business-line wording constrains what the ERC can later register, and how difficult it is to broaden scope after the fact compared with scoping it correctly the first time. For regulated industries — anything touching finance, education, healthcare, logistics, or e-commerce, among others — a further layer of sub-licensing or conditional business certificates typically follows ERC issuance before the company can actually commence the licensed activity, and building this into the project timeline from the outset avoids the common mistake of assuming the ERC is the finish line.

Industrial-zone setup and Export Processing Enterprise status

Manufacturing investors face a location decision that is inseparable from the licensing pathway. Registering inside an industrial or export processing zone generally streamlines land access and infrastructure and can shorten certain approval steps, since the zone management board handles functions that would otherwise sit with provincial authorities — but it also means operating under the zone’s own rules on customs treatment and reporting. Setting up outside a zone gives more flexibility on location and layout but shifts more of the administrative burden back onto the company itself. Layered on top of this is the question of Export Processing Enterprise status, which is built for businesses that export substantially all of their output and rewards that profile with customs and tax treatment closer to an offshore entity. The trade-off is real: EPE status typically requires physical separation from non-EPE operations, tighter customs recordkeeping, and constraints on selling into the domestic market without triggering additional duties. For a genuine export-only manufacturer, that trade-off is usually worth it; for a business that expects meaningful domestic sales alongside exports, standard FIE registration is often the better fit, and this is a decision worth pressure-testing against realistic sales projections rather than an idealized export plan.

Every one of these decisions is easier to make correctly before an application is filed than to correct afterward. Vietoria works through entity structure, ownership pathway, and licensing sequence with investors before paperwork begins, so the entity that gets registered is the one the business actually needs three years from now, not just on day one.
