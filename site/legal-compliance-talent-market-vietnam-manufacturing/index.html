Why Hiring a Senior Legal & Compliance Manager for a Vietnam Factory Is Getting Harder

A market analysis of senior legal and compliance hiring for Vietnam manufacturing sector — why the bar keeps rising and how companies should respond.

This isn’t a problem unique to one company — it reflects a genuine talent-market trend, where the bar for this role is rising faster than the supply of candidates who can clear it.

What’s changing in the hiring requirements

The Legal & Compliance Manager role for manufacturing operations used to be seen largely as an administrative-legal function — handling contracts, tracking licences. Today’s requirements have expanded considerably, reflecting three trends occurring at once:

ESG pressure from customers and overseas parent companies now requires this role to understand not just Vietnamese law but the international compliance standards imposed by customers or headquarters — a layer of requirement that wasn’t widely present five to seven years ago.

Rising frequency and complexity of inspections — in environment, fire safety, and labor — require someone who not only knows the law but has practical experience dealing with local regulators.

Deeper integration into international supply chains means this role increasingly has to work directly, in English, with headquarters and overseas partners — an additional language requirement layered onto an already narrow pool of qualified candidates.

These three trends compound into a profile that’s increasingly hard to fill: manufacturing experience + legal knowledge + English fluency + willingness to work at an industrial zone outside the city center. The pool of candidates meeting all four criteria at once is growing far more slowly than the pace at which new FDI companies are entering the Vietnamese market.

Why a standard job posting struggles to reach the right candidates

Most candidates suited for this role are already employed and stable elsewhere — they aren’t actively job-hunting, so a public job posting (even on social media) typically reaches only the narrower pool of active job-seekers, who don’t necessarily have the right experience. Reaching passive candidates requires a different approach — industry referrals, professional networks, or a specialist recruiter who understands this specific candidate pool.

How companies should respond

Don’t wait for the “perfect candidate” before the legal function starts operating. Define the minimum scope of work that needs coverage now, and have a temporary arrangement (internal delegation with oversight, or outsourced support) while recruitment continues.

Reassess whether the job description is realistic. Some companies stack too many requirements into a single role — consider splitting off a specialized portion (deep ESG compliance, for example) to dedicated outside advisors rather than expecting one person to cover everything.

Consider a more flexible legal-function structure — combining one in-house role handling day-to-day operations with an outside advisory relationship for specialized matters (cross-border transactions, restructuring, complex disputes) — rather than expecting a single role to cover it all.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is this role harder to fill than other legal positions?

Because it stacks multiple criteria at once — legal knowledge, manufacturing-operations understanding, English fluency, willingness to work at an industrial zone — while the pool of candidates meeting all four is relatively narrow and mostly already employed elsewhere.

What should a company do while the role remains open?

Have a temporary arrangement (supervised internal delegation, or outsourced/interim advisory support) so weekly legal decisions still have an accountable owner, rather than leaving the function completely uncovered.

Should companies lower hiring standards to fill the role faster?

Not the standards tied directly to risk (legal knowledge, compliance experience) — instead, review non-essential criteria (for example, an experience requirement set higher than actually needed) to widen the pool of suitable candidates.

Is a “hybrid” legal function (in-house plus outside advisory) common?

This is an increasingly common structure for small-to-mid-size companies or single-factory operations — it allows day-to-day coverage without carrying the fixed cost of a full in-house legal team.

Does this hiring difficulty vary by industrial zone?

It can vary by geography and the concentration of FDI companies in a given area — industrial zones farther from the city center often struggle more to attract candidates even at comparable compensation levels.
