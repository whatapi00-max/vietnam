Why This Matters for Foreign Investors / Foreign Companies

Vietnam’s education and training market — spanning language centers, vocational skills training, STEM and soft-skills programs, and increasingly edtech platforms — has attracted significant interest from foreign investors and educators, particularly in urban districts of Ho Chi Minh City such as Thao Dien, An Phu and Thu Duc where demand for international-standard education services is strong. However, this sector is also one of the more tightly regulated areas of Vietnam’s services economy, with licensing conditions that vary depending on the specific business line, the age group served, and whether the entity is wholly foreign-owned or a joint venture.

Because education businesses interact directly with children, students, parents and staff, regulators and consumers alike tend to scrutinize claims, contracts and data handling more closely than in many other service sectors. In practice, this means that founders and operators who treat licensing, teacher documentation and advertising claims as secondary concerns may face avoidable disruption — from delayed license renewals to consumer complaints about refund policies. A proactive legal review at the outset, and periodically thereafter, may help align business operations with applicable requirements as the regulatory landscape evolves.

Key Legal and Compliance Issues

  1. Sector classification and licensing. Education-related activities in Vietnam are typically split into several regulatory categories (for example, short-term training/skills centers, foreign-language centers, vocational education, and formal schooling), each of which may carry different licensing authorities and conditions. Businesses should verify which category their offering falls into before finalizing a business plan, as misclassification may cause delays or the need to restructure activities.
  1. Foreign investment conditions. Depending on the specific education sub-sector, foreign ownership caps, minimum capital expectations, facility standards and curriculum approval requirements may apply. Foreign investors should review current market-access conditions for their specific business line, as these conditions may differ for language centers, vocational training, and formal international schools.
  1. Facility and operating conditions. Many education licenses are tied to specific premises meeting conditions on classroom size, fire safety, and student capacity. Any change of location or expansion to a second campus may require a fresh review of licensing conditions rather than a simple notification.
  1. Teacher and trainer documentation. Centers engaging foreign or Vietnamese teachers should maintain complete files on qualifications, teaching experience and, where relevant, criminal record checks, as these documents are often requested during inspections or work permit applications for foreign staff.
  1. Advertising and course claims. Marketing materials that reference certifications, guaranteed outcomes, partnerships with foreign universities, or exam pass rates should be reviewed carefully, since overstated or unverifiable claims may expose the business to consumer protection or advertising law risk.
  1. Student and customer contracts. Enrollment agreements, tuition payment schedules, and refund or withdrawal policies should be clearly documented and consistent with consumer protection expectations, particularly where prepayment for multi-month or multi-year courses is involved.
  1. Data and privacy handling. Education businesses collect and store personal data of students (and, for minors, their parents), including in some cases sensitive information such as health or academic performance records, which may trigger heightened data protection obligations.

Practical Risks for Management

  • CEO/Founders: Launching marketing campaigns or opening new branches before confirming licensing conditions may lead to enforcement action or reputational damage that is difficult to reverse.
  • CFO: Refund and cancellation policies that are inconsistent with consumer protection norms may create unplanned liabilities and disputes that affect cash flow forecasting.
  • HR/Country Manager: Incomplete teacher qualification files may delay work permit renewals or expose the center to penalties if a labor inspection occurs.
  • Board: Absence of a documented compliance framework for advertising claims and data handling may increase exposure in the event of a parent or regulator complaint, particularly as scrutiny of the education sector tends to be higher than average.

What Companies Should Review

  • Confirm which licensing category applies to the specific education services offered
  • Review foreign ownership and capital conditions applicable to the relevant business line
  • Verify facility compliance (classroom capacity, fire safety, signage) against license conditions
  • Audit teacher and trainer personnel files for completeness (degrees, experience letters, background checks)
  • Review marketing and advertising materials for compliance with consumer protection expectations
  • Review student/customer contracts, including refund and cancellation clauses
  • Assess data collection, storage and consent practices for student and parent information
  • Confirm work permit and labor contract alignment for foreign teaching staff

FAQ

Do foreign investors need a local partner to open a language center in Vietnam?
This depends on the specific business line and current market-access conditions; some education services permit wholly foreign-owned entities while others may require or favor joint ventures. A case-specific review is recommended before finalizing a structure.

Can an education business change its curriculum without notifying authorities?
In many cases changes to curriculum, especially for licensed training programs, should be reviewed against the original license conditions, as material changes may require an update or approval.

What documents are typically needed for a foreign teacher’s file?
Common documents include degree certificates, teaching experience letters, and in many cases a criminal record check, though specific requirements should be confirmed for the applicable teaching role and location.

How should refund policies be worded to reduce dispute risk?
Refund and cancellation terms should be clear, prominently disclosed at enrollment, and consistent with consumer protection expectations; vague or one-sided terms may increase the likelihood of complaints.

Does an edtech platform need the same licenses as a physical training center?
Not necessarily — the applicable licensing regime may depend on whether the platform delivers structured courses, issues certificates, or simply provides content, so this should be assessed on a case-by-case basis.

What data protection issues are specific to student data?
Because much student data relates to minors, businesses should consider parental consent practices, data minimization, and secure storage in addition to general data protection obligations applicable to customer data generally.

How often should an education business review its legal compliance?
Given the pace of regulatory change and the sensitivity of the sector, an annual review, or a review triggered by any material change in operations (new branch, new age group, new foreign teacher intake), is generally advisable.
