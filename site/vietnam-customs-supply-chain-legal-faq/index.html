Vietnam Customs and Supply Chain: The Legal Questions International Manufacturers Ask

Vietnam’s customs regime for manufacturing exporters is generally favourable — duty exemptions for manufacturing inputs, preferential tariff access under CPTPP and EVFTA, and EPE status that provides a streamlined customs environment for companies that exclusively export. However, the rules are specific, the documentation requirements are strict, and the consequences of non-compliance — retroactive duty assessment, loss of preferential status, and penalties — are material. The following FAQ covers the most commonly asked customs and supply chain legal questions from G20 manufacturing investors in Vietnam.

Import Duty Exemptions

What imports are duty-exempt for Vietnam manufacturing companies?

Vietnam’s tax law provides duty exemptions for: machinery, equipment, and transportation means imported to create fixed assets of a qualifying investment project (used in the production process, not for sale or internal administrative use); raw materials, supplies, and components that cannot be produced domestically and are imported for direct manufacturing; and goods imported by EPE companies (export processing enterprises) where 100% of output is exported. Exemption eligibility for each category has specific conditions: the asset-creation exemption requires the machinery to be directly used in production (not office or auxiliary equipment); the raw material exemption requires confirmation that domestic alternatives are unavailable or not of sufficient quality; and EPE exemption requires formal EPE status endorsed in the IRC.

The duty exemption must be planned before import — it is not generally claimable retrospectively after goods are imported under a standard paid-duty declaration. Companies that import manufacturing equipment or materials without confirming exemption eligibility first, and then attempt to reclaim duties already paid, face significant procedural obstacles and often cannot recover the duties.

What is EPE (Export Processing Enterprise) status and when should it be used?

An Export Processing Enterprise (EPE) is a foreign-invested enterprise that manufactures exclusively for export and operates within a customs-controlled zone or under equivalent customs supervision. EPE status provides: exemption from import duties on all raw materials, components, and machinery used in production; a simplified customs clearance regime for imports and exports; VAT exemption on goods and services exchanged between EPE companies; and the ability to purchase domestically produced goods as if the domestic purchase were an export (allowing the domestic supplier to export VAT on the sale). EPE status is appropriate for manufacturers whose 100% output is exported — companies that have any domestic sales cannot maintain EPE status without a dual-accounting and customs segregation arrangement that tracks export vs. domestic portions.

What are the most common customs duty mistakes Vietnam manufacturers make?

The three most consistent customs compliance failures for Vietnam manufacturers are: (1) HS code misclassification — using an incorrect tariff heading for imported inputs or exported goods, either through inadequate classification analysis or intentional misclassification, which leads to duty underpayment liability, preferential tariff claim invalidity, and origin certificate invalidity; (2) customs valuation understatement — declaring imported goods (particularly from related-party suppliers) at below-market values to reduce duty, which triggers valuation audits under Vietnam’s Customs Law and potential penalties; and (3) raw material exemption scope overreach — importing goods on a manufacturing exemption that do not actually qualify (for example, packaging materials that are not directly consumed in the manufactured product, or spare parts that exceed a reasonable quantity for factory maintenance). Each of these creates retroactive duty liability that can be material for manufacturers with high import volumes.

HS Code Classification

How should manufacturers confirm the correct HS code for their products?

Vietnam follows the ASEAN Harmonized Tariff Nomenclature (AHTN), which is based on the WCO Harmonized System. HS code classification requires analysis of the product’s essential character, manufacturing process, composition, and end use. For manufactured goods with complex compositions or multi-function capability (common in electronics and automotive components), the classification analysis can be non-obvious and should be conducted by a qualified customs specialist. Vietnam Customs offers a formal advance ruling (classification ruling) process through which manufacturers can obtain a binding customs classification decision before import — this provides certainty and protection against reclassification risk for high-volume or high-value imports.

Manufacturers who self-classify without professional analysis, or who use Chinese parent company HS codes without confirming applicability under Vietnam’s tariff schedule, frequently find that Vietnamese customs applies a different classification with a different duty rate, preferential treatment eligibility, or import restriction status. Classification audits can extend three years back under Vietnam’s statute of limitations for customs violations.

Rules of Origin Documentation

How does a Vietnam manufacturer certify origin for EVFTA exports to the EU?

Under EVFTA, Vietnam manufacturers can use self-certification of origin (Statement on Origin) for consignments above EUR 6,000 if they are registered as a REX (Registered Exporter) with Vietnam Customs. For consignments below EUR 6,000, a Statement on Origin can be made on any commercial document without REX registration. The Statement on Origin must include the specific EVFTA origin statement text, the REX number (where applicable), and the exporter’s signature. The manufacturer must be able to substantiate the origin claim — through a Bill of Materials showing the origin and HS classification of all inputs, production records, and value content calculations — if requested by EU customs authorities.

Manufacturers exporting under EVFTA who have not completed their REX registration, or who issue Statements on Origin without the supporting documentation chain, risk retroactive loss of preferential duty treatment for all related shipments — which can be retrospective three to four years under EU customs audit timelines. REX registration should be completed before the first EVFTA shipment, not after.

Supply Chain Contracts

What legal provisions are critical in Vietnam supply and procurement contracts?

Supply contracts with Vietnam suppliers or customers require specific provisions that differ from international standard templates. Key provisions include: governing law (Vietnamese law applies to contracts performed in Vietnam — but the parties can agree on foreign law for international contracts, subject to Vietnamese public policy limits); dispute resolution (specifying either Vietnamese court jurisdiction or international arbitration, with enforceability considerations in both directions); payment terms (specifying the currency, bank transfer requirement, and FCT implications for cross-border payments); VAT invoice requirements (requiring the supplier to issue compliant VAT e-invoices on time — non-compliant invoices are not VAT-deductible); and force majeure definitions that reflect Vietnam operational realities (power outages, provincial licensing delays, public health measures) rather than only international commercial force majeure events.

How should quality warranty and liability provisions be adapted for Vietnam supply contracts?

International quality warranty and liability templates often do not translate directly to Vietnamese supply chain contexts. Key adaptations include: warranty periods calibrated to Vietnam commercial practice and the specific product type (rather than global template standards); warranty claim procedures that are realistic for Vietnam logistics and inspection timelines; liability caps that reflect the supplier’s financial capacity (a Vietnam SME supplier that cannot pay a multi-million-dollar liability cap provides no real protection — a realistic cap and performance bond or insurance requirement provides more practical protection); and termination provisions with transition assistance obligations to manage supply continuity risk. Buyers who apply global procurement templates to Vietnam supplier relationships consistently face enforcement gaps when disputes arise — because the template assumptions (court jurisdiction, credit-worthy suppliers, standard commercial practice) do not match Vietnam realities.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a Vietnam company import goods duty-free and then sell them domestically?

No — duty exemptions for manufacturing inputs and EPE imports are conditional on the goods being used in the manufacturing process for export production. If a company imports duty-free raw materials or components but sells them domestically (either as materials or as finished goods incorporating them), the duty exemption is retroactively disallowed and the company owes the original duties plus penalties. The distinction between imported materials used in domestic-sale production and those used in export production must be tracked and documented — this is particularly important for dual-purpose manufacturers (those producing both for domestic sale and export) where the correct duty treatment of each input stream must be carefully separated.

What CPTPP tariff rates apply to Vietnam exports to Japan and Canada?

Under CPTPP, most Vietnamese manufactured goods can access substantially reduced or zero tariff rates in Japan and Canada — subject to rules of origin compliance. Japan has eliminated tariffs on a broad range of Vietnamese manufactured goods on either a Year 1 or phased schedule under CPTPP. Canada has similarly reduced or eliminated tariffs on manufactured goods from Vietnam. The applicable tariff rate for a specific product depends on the HS code, the importing country’s CPTPP tariff schedule, and whether the goods qualify for preferential treatment under the applicable product-specific rules of origin. Manufacturers should confirm the specific tariff rate and origin rule for their product category before planning their export pricing — the preferential tariff benefit varies significantly by product.

What should a Vietnam manufacturer do if customs reclassifies imported goods at a higher duty rate?

A customs reclassification decision can be contested through the formal customs appeal process: first to the customs supervisor at the point of entry, then to the General Department of Customs, and ultimately to an administrative court if the reclassification is not reversed. The appeal must be supported by a professional classification analysis, technical product specifications, and, where appropriate, a WCO classification ruling or comparable customs authority ruling from another jurisdiction. Advance classification rulings (obtainable before import) provide the most effective protection against reclassification — a manufacturer with a valid advance ruling is substantially better positioned in a reclassification dispute than one relying on self-classification.
