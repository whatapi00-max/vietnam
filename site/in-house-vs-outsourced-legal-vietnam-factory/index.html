In-House Legal Manager or Outsourced Legal Services for Your Vietnam Factory?

Compare the cost, speed and risk trade-offs between hiring an in-house Legal & Compliance Manager and using outsourced or interim legal services for a Vietnam factory.

Why this role is so hard to fill

A job description for a factory-level Legal & Compliance Manager typically stacks several demanding criteria at once: 7-10 years of experience, familiarity with both investment law and manufacturing operations (fire safety, environmental compliance, occupational safety), fluent English for coordination with an overseas headquarters, and willingness to work on-site at an industrial zone — often well outside the city center. The pool of candidates who meet all four criteria simultaneously is narrow, and most of them are already employed elsewhere (passive candidates), which means a standard job posting typically reaches only the smaller pool of candidates actively looking, not necessarily the ones with the right experience. The practical result: many companies spend two to three months, sometimes longer, filling this role — while contractual volume and compliance exposure keep accumulating every week regardless.

Comparing three options

1. Full-time in-house hire. Suits companies with a legal workload large and steady enough to keep a full-time position genuinely busy (multiple factories, high contract volume, frequent inspections). The fixed monthly cost (salary, benefits, onboarding) doesn’t flex with workload — worth weighing, not an absolute drawback, since it comes with deep institutional knowledge an outsourced provider cannot replicate on day one.

2. Outsourced Legal & Compliance services. Suits companies whose workload is uneven — heavier in some months (an inspection cycle, a major contract negotiation) and lighter in others — and that would rather have costs flex with actual demand than carry a fixed headcount cost. This gives access to a team that has already handled similar situations across multiple factories, but still requires an internal point of contact with enough authority to coordinate and decide quickly — outsourcing does not remove the need for someone who owns day-to-day operations internally.

3. Interim Legal Officer. Suits specifically the “gap period” — while an in-house search is still running. An experienced lawyer or compliance advisor takes on the role temporarily, so weekly legal decisions still have an accountable owner, while handing over processes and files to the eventual permanent hire. This is not a long-term substitute; it’s a bridge so legal decisions don’t freeze while recruitment continues.

In practice, many companies don’t pick one option exclusively — they combine them: use interim or outsourced support during the transition, then hire in-house once the real workload is clear, while retaining an outsourced relationship for specialized matters (cross-border transactions, restructuring) that a single in-house role would struggle to cover on its own.

The real risk during the “gap period”

Board/CEO face the risk that no one is tracking compliance deadlines (licence renewals, periodic environmental reporting) if the responsible role sits vacant too long.

HR Director carries dual pressure: recruiting the right candidate while also explaining to leadership why the legal front is currently uncovered.

CFO needs to weigh not just recruiting cost (headhunter fees, time-to-hire) but the opportunity cost of a legal issue surfacing with no one positioned to respond promptly.

Practical steps

Assess actual legal workload over the past 12 months (number of contracts, inspection cycles, labor/environmental issues that arose) before deciding in-house versus outsourced — don’t decide based on a general sense that “we need someone.” If an in-house search has run 4-6 weeks without results, consider an interim arrangement to avoid a coverage gap rather than continuing to wait without a fallback plan. Identify which work genuinely requires an on-site presence (supervising fire-safety inspections, handling regulator visits) versus what can be handled remotely (drafting contracts, structuring advice) — this directly informs the in-house-versus-outsourced decision.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can outsourced legal services fully replace an in-house Legal Manager?

Not entirely — outsourcing works best where workload is uneven or during a transition period; companies with a large, steady legal workload still benefit from someone in-house managing day-to-day operations.

How long should an Interim Legal Officer engagement typically run?

Usually tied to the company’s actual time-to-hire — commonly a few weeks to a few months, until the in-house role is filled and handover is complete.

Is outsourcing cheaper than hiring in-house?

It depends on actual workload — for uneven demand, cost that flexes with need is typically more efficient than a fixed full-time monthly cost; for large, steady workloads, the comparison can go the other way. This should be assessed using the past 12 months of data, not general assumptions.

Can a company move from outsourced to in-house later?

Yes — this is a common pattern: use outsourced or interim support early to establish the real workload, then decide on an in-house hire based on actual data rather than initial estimates.

Does an Interim Legal Officer need to be on-site at the factory?

Depends on scope — some work (regulator visits, fire-safety supervision) requires on-site presence, while other work (contract drafting, structuring advice) can be handled remotely; this should be defined before engagement.
